Reading Room

Twelve papers on pneumonia detection from chest X-rays. Accuracy on the Kermany pediatric CXR benchmark climbed from 92.8% to 97.6% in six years — and we found that nobody checked whether the models were looking at the lungs.

Pneumonia (Chest X-ray) · Kermany CXR10 min · 9 slides + Q&A appendix
Opening (any speaker, 30s): Our topic is pneumonia classification on the Kermany chest X-ray dataset. We read 12 papers. The headline: reported accuracy went up ~5 points in six years, but not one paper on this benchmark verified that the gain is real or that the model uses lung pathology. That unverified gain is our research gap. Each of us takes one slide of three papers.
01 · The benchmark everyone reports on

5,856 X-rays. One hospital. Ages 1–5.

Kermany et al. released this set with the 2018 Cell paper. It became the default pneumonia benchmark — and its shape explains most of what we found.

Train5,216
Test624
Validation16

The official validation split is 16 images — 8 per class. It cannot rank two models. In practice every paper tunes against the 624-image test set, then reports on it.

Where it comes from

Guangzhou Women and Children's Medical Center, China. Pediatric patients aged 1–5. One site, one scanner fleet, one acquisition protocol, one population.

What the label means

4,273 pneumonia / 1,583 normal — a 2.7 : 1 imbalance. Always predicting "pneumonia" scores 62.5% on the test set, so accuracy alone is a weak claim.

Why the size matters

624 test images gives a 95% confidence interval of roughly ±1.5 percentage points. A "1% improvement" is six images.

The benchmarkAny speaker · 60 s
Say (60s): Three numbers to hold onto. 5,216 train / 624 test / 16 validation. One hospital in Guangzhou, children aged one to five. 2.7:1 imbalance — so a model that always says "pneumonia" gets 62.5%. And 624 test images means a 95% CI of about ±1.5 points. Every number you see on the next four slides was measured on those same 624 images. If asked "why does 16 matter?" — model selection (early stopping, LR, architecture choice) needs a held-out set. With 16 images you cannot distinguish 93% from 87%. So people select on test, which inflates the reported number.
02 · Track A — Foundations

The three papers the whole field is built on

2017–2018: the datasets appear, transfer learning is shown to work, and "radiologist-level" enters the vocabulary.

P1 · Base paper candidate
Identifying Medical Diagnoses by Image-Based Deep Learning
Kermany et al. · Cell 2018 · ~6,100 cites
92.8% acc · 93.2% sens · 90.1% spec

ImageNet-pretrained Inception-V3, convolutional layers frozen and the head retrained. Also separated bacterial vs viral pneumonia at 90.7%.

Gave us the dataset and the baseline. Reports one number, one test set.
P2 · Method
CheXNet: Radiologist-Level Pneumonia Detection
Rajpurkar et al. · arXiv 2017 · Stanford ML Group
F1 0.435 vs radiologists 0.387

121-layer DenseNet on ChestX-ray14. Introduced the Grad-CAM heatmap as the standard way to "explain" a CXR model. One of the few to report bootstrap 95% CIs.

Set the architecture template. An F1 of 0.44 is far from solved.
P3 · Dataset & scale
ChestX-ray8: Hospital-Scale Database and Benchmarks
Wang et al. · CVPR 2017 · NIH
108,948 images · 32,717 patients

Labels text-mined from radiology reports by NLP, not read from images — so labels are noisy. Ships bounding boxes and scores localization, not just classification.

The one early paper that measured where the model looks.
FoundationsSamarth Shekhar · 230042 · 2 min
Samarth (2 min): Kermany is our likely base paper — Inception-V3, frozen conv layers, retrained head, 92.8/93.2/90.1. Simple, public data, a clear target to reproduce. CheXNet is the architecture template everyone copied: DenseNet-121, and it's where Grad-CAM heatmaps became the standard explanation. Note CheXNet's actual pneumonia F1 is 0.435 — beating radiologists at 0.387, but both are low; pneumonia is genuinely hard. ChestX-ray8 matters for two reasons: its labels are NLP-mined from reports so they're noisy, and it's the only early paper that scored *localization* with bounding boxes rather than just showing pretty heatmaps. Hard question — "why is CheXNet's F1 so low if accuracy elsewhere is 97%?" Different dataset and task: ChestX-ray14 is adult, multi-label, 14 pathologies, noisy NLP labels. Kermany is pediatric, binary, clean, single-site — much easier. That difficulty gap is itself evidence the Kermany number is optimistic.
03 · Track B — The accuracy race

Everyone swaps the backbone; everyone reports higher

2019–2020: the same 624 test images, a different pretrained network each time. All three papers below use the Kermany data.

P4 · From scratch
An Efficient Deep Learning Approach to Pneumonia Classification
Stephen et al. · J. Healthcare Eng. 2019 · ~800 cites
CNN trained from scratch + heavy augmentation

Argues you don't need ImageNet at all — a purpose-built CNN plus rotation, zoom and flip augmentation reaches competitive accuracy on 5,856 images.

Augmentation ≈ pretraining, at this scale. No CI, no external test.
P5 · Head-to-head
Diagnosis of Pneumonia from Chest X-Ray Images Using Deep Learning
Ayan & Ünver · IEEE EBBT 2019 · ~550 cites
VGG16 87% acc · Xception 82% acc

The useful result is the split: VGG16 wins on specificity (91% vs 76%), Xception wins on sensitivity (85% vs 82%). Different networks make different mistakes.

The honest baseline — and the argument for ensembles. Nobody explains why.
P6 · Backbone sweep
Transfer Learning with Deep CNN for Pneumonia Detection
Rahman et al. · Applied Sciences 2020 · ~815 cites
DenseNet-201 · 98% normal-vs-pneumonia

Compares AlexNet, ResNet-18, DenseNet-201 and SqueezeNet. Drops to 93.3% on bacterial vs viral — the clinically useful question is much harder.

Best headline number of the group. Single split, single seed.
The accuracy raceMeghavi Rao · 230044 · 2 min
Meghavi (2 min): These three are the pattern of the whole field. Stephen shows you can skip ImageNet entirely if you augment hard — useful, because it means the gains aren't really about pretraining. Ayan & Ünver is the most honest paper here: they publish 87% and 82%, and the interesting bit is the trade-off — VGG16 is specific (91%), Xception is sensitive (85%). Different architectures make different errors, which is exactly the argument for the ensembles on the next slide. Rahman sweeps four backbones, DenseNet-201 wins at 98% — but look at the second number: bacterial vs viral drops to 93.3%, and that's the question a doctor actually needs answered, because it decides whether you give antibiotics. Hard question — "isn't 98% basically solved?" No: it's 98% on 624 images from one hospital, with no CI, one seed, one split. That's the entire point of slide 7.
04 · Track C — Ensembles, explanation, transformers

Three ways to push past 96% on the same 624 images

Combine models, visualise the decision, or replace convolution entirely.

P7 · Ensemble
A Novel Transfer Learning Based Approach for Pneumonia Detection
Chouhan et al. · Applied Sciences 2020 · ~890 cites
96.4% acc · 99.62% recall

Ensembles five backbones — AlexNet, DenseNet-121, Inception-V3, ResNet-18, GoogLeNet. Highest recall we found, which is the metric that matters for screening.

Confirms Ayan's diversity argument. 5× the compute for +1 point.
P8 · Interpretability
Visualization and Interpretation of CNN Predictions in Pediatric CXR
Rajaraman et al. · Applied Sciences 2018 · ~400 cites
Customized VGG16 · 96.2% acc · 93.6% bact/viral

The one Kermany paper that takes explanation seriously — CAM visualisations of what drives each prediction, plus a customised, trimmed VGG16.

Shows heatmaps. Never scores them against a radiologist.
P9 · Current SOTA
Efficient Pneumonia Detection Using Vision Transformers on Chest X-Rays
Singh et al. · Scientific Reports 2024
97.61% acc · ~95% sens · ~98% spec

Self-attention over image patches instead of convolution, arguing ViT captures global context a CNN's local receptive field misses.

The highest number on this benchmark. Same 624 images, still no CI.
Ensembles & transformersRachit Kumar · 230128 · 2 min
Rachit (2 min): Chouhan ensembles five networks and gets 96.4% accuracy with 99.62% recall — recall is the number you want for screening, because a missed pneumonia is far worse than a false alarm. But it's five models for about one point over a single DenseNet. Rajaraman is the interpretability paper: 96.2%, and importantly it actually shows CAM visualisations of what the network keyed on. That's the closest anyone on this dataset gets to checking the model's reasoning — and it's still only pictures in a figure, never scored. Singh 2024 is the current top: a Vision Transformer at 97.61%. Self-attention sees the whole image at once instead of building up from local patches. Hard question — "why would a ViT beat a CNN on 5,000 images? ViTs need huge data." Correct, and that's a real tension — it only works because it's pretrained on ImageNet-21k and fine-tuned. Trained from scratch on 5,216 images a ViT would lose badly to a CNN. There's also a cost: reported comparisons put ViT inference around two orders of magnitude slower than a small CNN on CPU.
05 · Track D — The reality check

Three papers that tested whether any of this holds up

Published in PLOS Medicine and Nature Machine Intelligence — and none of them uses the Kermany dataset. That disconnect is our gap.

P10 · Generalisation
Variable Generalization Performance of a Deep Learning Model
Zech et al. · PLOS Medicine 2018
AUC 0.931 internal → 0.815 external

Trained on Mount Sinai + NIH, tested at Indiana. Performance collapses off-site. The CNN identified which hospital an X-ray came from with 99.95% accuracy, and separated portable from fixed scanners perfectly.

It learned the hospital, not the disease.
P11 · Shortcut learning
AI for Radiographic COVID-19 Detection Selects Shortcuts Over Signal
DeGrave, Janizek & Lee · Nature Mach. Intell. 2021 · ~980 cites
High in-distribution AUC → fails externally

Names the mechanism. Models key on laterality markers, image borders, collimation edges and patient positioning — everything outside the lungs — because those correlate with the label in a single-source dataset.

Exactly the failure a single-hospital dataset invites.
P12 · Explanations
Benchmarking Saliency Methods for Chest X-Ray Interpretation
Saporta et al. · Nature Mach. Intell. 2022 · CheXlocalize
All 7 saliency methods < human benchmark

Scored seven methods against expert segmentations using mIoU and hit rate. Grad-CAM was the best of the seven — and still significantly worse than a radiologist. Worst on small, complex findings.

A convincing heatmap is not evidence.
The reality checkHarshal Nerpagar · 230076 · 2 min
Harshal (2 min): These three are why we're not just going to chase 98%. Zech is the clearest result in the whole review: a model at AUC 0.931 in-house drops to 0.815 at another hospital — and the same network could tell you *which hospital* an X-ray came from with 99.95% accuracy, and portable-vs-fixed scanner with 100%. It learned the site. DeGrave names the mechanism — shortcut learning: laterality markers, borders, collimation edges, how the patient is positioned. All outside the lungs, all correlated with the label when your data comes from one place. Saporta closes it: they scored seven saliency methods against radiologist segmentations. Grad-CAM was best of the seven and still significantly worse than a human. So all those Grad-CAM figures in papers P1–P9 are not evidence the model looked at the lungs — they were never measured. Hard question — "but Kermany is one hospital, so there's no site confound." That's worse, not better: with a single site there's no signal telling the model to ignore acquisition artifacts, so shortcuts are free. And nobody has tested it, because nobody externally validates on this dataset.

07 · Research gap

The gap none of the twelve papers closes

Every paper on this benchmark reports a single accuracy number on the same 624 single-centre images. Every paper that tested whether such models actually work used a different dataset. Nobody has run the reality check on the benchmark itself.

GAP 01

Selection on the test set

A 16-image validation split cannot rank models, so tuning happens against the 624 test images. Reported numbers are optimistic by construction.

GAP 02

No error bars

Zero of the nine benchmark papers report a CI or run multiple seeds. Every gain claimed since 2019 — 96.2 → 98.0 — sits inside the noise window.

GAP 03

No external validation

One hospital, one city, ages 1–5. Zech showed −0.12 AUC off-site. Nobody has measured that drop for this dataset.

GAP 04

No calibration

Not one paper reports ECE or a reliability curve. A triage tool needs a trustworthy confidence, not just a correct label.

GAP 05

Heatmaps never scored

Grad-CAM appears as figures in most papers. Saporta showed scoring changes the verdict. Nobody scores them here.

Research gapAny speaker · 90 s — the bonus-point slide
Say (90s): State the claim in the box verbatim, then walk the five. The one-sentence version if you're short on time: "The Kermany literature optimises a number; the critique literature questions whether that kind of number means anything — and the two have never been put in the same experiment." Hard question — "isn't 'nobody did external validation' just a dataset limitation, not a research gap?" The gap isn't that the dataset is single-centre — it's that the field kept publishing increasingly precise-sounding numbers on it for six years without ever quantifying what that limitation costs. Nobody has said how much of the 97.6% is lung pathology and how much is acquisition artifact. That's a measurable, unanswered question, and it's what we propose to measure. "Has really nobody done this?" Be honest: individual pieces exist — people have re-split the data, and shortcut learning is studied on COVID datasets. What we found no paper doing is the combination: on this benchmark, with a proper split, with error bars, testing whether the decision depends on the lung field.
08 · Where we go next

Reproduce Kermany, then test whether it needs the lungs

M2 · Reproduce

Kermany et al. 2018, Inception-V3

Public dataset, ImageNet weights, a clearly stated target: 92.8% acc, 93.2% sens, 90.1% spec. Small enough to train on a free GPU, well-specified enough to actually match.

Fallback if reproduction stalls: Rahman 2020 (DenseNet-201) — same data, more implementations available.

M3 · Our one change

Train and test on lung-cropped X-rays

Segment the lung fields and crop. Everything the shortcut literature blames — laterality markers, collimation edges, borders, positioning — is outside that crop. Identical model, identical schedule, only the input region changes.

Prediction — submitted before we code Standard test accuracy will move less than 1 point (no free lunch). But under a context-ablation test — lungs masked out, periphery kept — the full-image model will stay well above the 62.5% majority baseline while the cropped model falls toward it. That gap is the shortcut, measured.
M4 · M5 · Measure honestly

The evaluation the field skipped

  • Patient-wise re-split — a real validation set, not 16 images
  • 5 seeds, mean ± 95% CI on every number we report
  • Sensitivity / recall + AUC, never accuracy alone
  • ECE + reliability curve — the first calibration numbers on this benchmark
  • Grad-CAM hit rate inside the lung field, scored, not shown

Ablations: crop only at test, crop only at train, and full-image control.

Next milestonesAny speaker · 90 s
Say (90s): Base paper is Kermany — public data, ImageNet Inception-V3, and a clear three-number target to land near. Our one change is deliberately simple: segment the lungs and crop, so everything the shortcut literature blames is physically removed from the input. Same model, same schedule, only the input region changes. Our prediction, which we're committing to before coding: standard accuracy barely moves — under a point — because the lungs carry the real signal. The interesting result is the stress test: mask the lungs out and keep only the periphery. If the full-image model still scores well above the 62.5% majority baseline on lung-free images, it was using shortcuts, and we'll have measured how much. Around that we run the evaluation nobody ran: proper patient-wise split, five seeds with confidence intervals, sensitivity and AUC not just accuracy, calibration error, and a Grad-CAM hit rate scored inside the lung mask. Hard question — "what if the prediction is wrong and cropping hurts accuracy a lot?" That's still a result, and the brief says being wrong is fine if the reasoning is good. If cropping costs several points, it means clinically-relevant signal lives outside our mask — most likely the costophrenic angles and retrocardiac region, which segmentation models routinely clip. We'd report that as a finding about the segmentation, and it's a real limitation to write up. "Where does the lung mask come from?" A pretrained CXR lung segmentation model (U-Net trained on Montgomery/Shenzhen) — we're not hand-annotating, and we'll report mask quality as a limitation.
Appendix · not presented — Q&A backup

If you get pushed on something

"Why this base paper and not the 97.6% ViT?"

Reproducibility, not performance. Kermany has public data, a stated architecture, and three stated metrics. We need a baseline we can actually land near, then change one thing.

"How do you know the models use shortcuts here?"

We don't — that's the point. It's shown on other CXR datasets (Zech, DeGrave). Whether it happens on Kermany is an open question, and it's the one we're measuring.

"Bacterial vs viral — why does it matter?"

It decides antibiotics. Every paper scores worse on it (90.7% → 93.6%) than on normal-vs-pneumonia, because the visual difference is far subtler.

"Why is accuracy a bad metric here?"

2.7 : 1 imbalance. Predicting "pneumonia" for everything scores 62.5%. We report sensitivity/recall and AUC, per the project rules.

"What is calibration and why bring it up?"

Whether a "90% confident" prediction is right 90% of the time. Softmax networks are famously overconfident. Zero of our twelve papers measure it.

"Isn't Grad-CAM already an explanation?"

Saporta 2022 scored seven saliency methods against radiologist segmentations. Grad-CAM led — and was still significantly worse than a human. A heatmap is a hypothesis, not evidence.

"Isn't this dataset basically saturated?"

Saturated on one metric. It is unmeasured on generalisation, calibration and localisation — which is the difference between a leaderboard and a tool.

"You said gains are noise — is Kermany→98% not real then?"

The first jump is real: 92.8 → 96.2 clears the ±1.5 pt window. What is not separable is everything after — 96.2, 96.4, 97.6, 98.0 all overlap. Five years, no measurable gain.

"What are the limitations of your own plan?"

The lung mask is model-generated and can clip costophrenic angles. We still have no external test set. And 624 test images limits what any of our CIs can resolve.
